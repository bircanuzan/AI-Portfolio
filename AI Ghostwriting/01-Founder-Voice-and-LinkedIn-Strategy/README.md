# Founder Voice & LinkedIn Content Strategy
**Independent speculative portfolio sample | Prepared for Bircan Uzan | November 2026**

## Brief
Demonstrate the multi-client voice adaptation, hooks, storytelling and inbound-oriented content required by freelance LinkedIn ghostwriting roles. Fictional client: founder of a B2B workflow startup. Audience: operations leaders at growing companies. No client commission, interview, publication or measured results are claimed.

## Voice brief
Practical, candid, slightly skeptical. Short explanations, concrete operational examples. Avoid grand predictions, manufactured success stories and invented personal anecdotes. Central belief: automation is useful only when people can handle exceptions.

## Discovery questions
1. Which customer problem do you explain repeatedly?
2. What belief has changed as you built the product?
3. What can we substantiate with approved evidence?
4. Which subjects and customer details are off limits?
5. What should a qualified reader do after reading?

## Three content pillars
- Operational judgment: when a workflow should pause.
- Building in public: explain a real design choice once approved.
- Buyer education: how to assess automation beyond a demo.

## Finished LinkedIn post 1
A workflow that never stops can be a liability.

Imagine an invoice approval process.

The supplier name looks familiar. The amount is within the limit. The system moves it forward.

But the bank details changed yesterday.

That is the moment a good workflow should become less autonomous.

It should show the change, explain why it matters and route the decision to someone with the authority to approve it.

The useful design question is not simply, “Can we automate this?”

It is:

What should the system recognize?
When should it pause?
Who owns the next decision?

Those questions belong in the product brief before they become an incident review.

If you are assessing an automation tool, ask to see one messy exception alongside the happy path.

The answer will tell you more than another flawless demo.

## Finished LinkedIn post 2
Your automation demo needs an exception.

Ask the vendor to change one detail halfway through the workflow.

Remove a required input.
Introduce conflicting instructions.
Make the approval service unavailable.

Then watch what happens.

Does the system explain the problem?
Does it preserve the work already completed?
Can a person recover without starting again?
Is the final decision recorded?

A polished demo shows what a product can do when everything cooperates.

An exception shows how the product behaves when your business does not.

Both deserve time in the buying process.

Which exception would you put in the demo?
